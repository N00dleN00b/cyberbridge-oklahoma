# What China Taught Me About Cybersecurity and AI

*By Bristie Rahman · Part of my Gilman Scholarship Follow-on Service Project*

Studying in China, at Shanghai University of Sport and China University of Petroleum (Beijing), showed me that the internet I use every day is only one version of the internet.

## Daily digital life looks different

A few things stand out to almost anyone who spends time in China:
- **Paying by phone is everywhere.** Scanning a QR code with your phone is a normal way to pay for everyday purchases.
- **One app can do almost everything.** "Super apps" combine messaging, payments, rides and more.
- **Many apps Americans rely on are blocked or limited**, and Chinese platforms fill those roles. Before you go, you have to think about how you'll stay in touch with family.

As someone who works in security, what I kept coming back to was the tradeoff: the more convenient one app makes your life, the more data it holds about you. Americans make that same tradeoff every day, just with different apps.

## Different rules for data

China has national laws covering cybersecurity, data security and personal information. The U.S. takes a different approach: no single national privacy law, but a mix of federal rules for specific areas and state laws. Comparing the two made me think harder about who decides how our data is used, and who should.

## AI is a global field

Sitting in on sample AI lectures in China reminded me that AI and cybersecurity are worldwide fields. The models we use are trained on data from around the world, researchers collaborate across borders, and attacks can cross the globe in seconds. To protect our communities, we need people who understand how technology works everywhere, not just at home.

## Bringing it home to Oklahoma

Back in Oklahoma, the threats feel close to home: phishing emails aimed at school district staff, data breaches at hospitals, and scam calls and texts targeting families, now sometimes with AI-cloned voices.

That's what I built **CyberBridge Oklahoma** around. It's three free modules, "AI in Everyday Life," "Understanding Security Controls," and "Pathways to Cybersecurity Careers," plus a lesson on what I learned in China and a scam-spotting game called Phishing Spotter. Teachers, librarians and outreach teams can use all of it for free.

## One tip if you travel abroad

Update your devices, turn on multi-factor authentication (and make sure you can get your codes overseas), back up your data, be careful on public Wi-Fi, and learn the local rules about apps before you go.
