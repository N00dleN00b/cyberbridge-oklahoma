# Global Perspectives: What I Learned in China — Lesson Plan

**Ages:** 13+ and adults · **Time:** 30 minutes · **Prep:** 10 minutes

## Purpose
Connect global knowledge to local needs. Participants compare digital life in China and the U.S., think critically about privacy and technology, and see study abroad, and the Gilman Scholarship, as something open to them.

> **Facilitator note:** this lesson is built on Bristie Rahman's Gilman program in China. If you're a different facilitator, tell it as "the creator of this curriculum…" or swap in your own international experience. Present differences with curiosity and respect, not as "better" or "worse," and let participants form their own opinions.

## Learning objectives
Participants will be able to:
1. Describe two ways everyday digital life in China differs from the U.S.
2. Compare, at a high level, how the two countries approach data and privacy rules.
3. Explain one thing travelers should do to protect their devices and data abroad.
4. Name the Gilman Scholarship and one way to learn more about studying abroad.

## Background: Bristie's program
Through the Gilman Scholarship, Bristie studied in China and traveled to several cities. She attended **Shanghai University of Sport** and **China University of Petroleum (Beijing)**, learned about a range of topics, sat in on **sample AI lectures**, and visited landmarks including the **Forbidden City** and the **Great Wall**.

## Lesson flow

### 1. Where in the world? (5 min)
Show a simple map of China with Beijing and Shanghai marked. Share the journey: two universities in two very different cities, AI lectures, the Forbidden City (the imperial palace at the center of Beijing) and the Great Wall.
Ask: *"Has anyone here been outside Oklahoma? Outside the U.S.? What would you most want to see?"*

### 2. Same internet? Not quite (10 min)
Things visitors to China commonly notice about digital life:
- **Paying by phone.** Mobile payment by scanning a QR code is used for everyday purchases far more widely than in the U.S.
- **Super apps.** A single app is often used for messaging, payments, booking rides and more.
- **Different platforms.** Many apps and sites Americans use daily are blocked or don't work normally in China, and Chinese platforms fill those roles. Travelers have to plan how to stay in touch with family.
- **Different rules for data.** China has national laws covering cybersecurity (2017), data security (2021) and personal information protection (2021). The U.S. has no single national privacy law; protection comes from federal rules for specific areas (like student records and children's data) plus state laws.
- **The same tradeoff everywhere.** Convenience (fast payments, one app for everything) vs. how much data gets collected and who can see it. Americans make this tradeoff too, just with different apps.

Pair discussion (2 min): *"Would you trade some privacy for more convenience? Where's your line?"*

### 3. AI is a global field (5 min)
Bristie attended sample AI lectures while abroad. Key message: AI and cybersecurity are worldwide fields. Researchers, companies and attackers work across borders, and attacks can travel around the world in seconds. Understanding how other countries build and regulate technology makes you better at protecting your own community.
Connect to Module 1: *"The AI apps you use were trained on data from all over the world. Whose data, and whose rules?"*

### 4. Traveler's security tips (4 min)
Wherever you travel:
- Update your phone and laptop before you go; turn on multi-factor authentication and make sure you can still get codes abroad
- Back up your data before travel
- Be careful on public Wi-Fi; avoid logging into sensitive accounts on shared computers
- Learn the local rules for apps and data before you go, and follow them
- Bring only the data you need

### 5. Study abroad is for you (5 min)
Use `../../workshops/gilman-segment.md`.

### 6. Reflection (1 min)
*"One thing from another country I'd want to see in Oklahoma, and one thing I wouldn't, is ____."*

## Respect and accuracy notes
- Speak from experience rather than generalizing about 1.4 billion people.
- Keep the focus on digital life, data and learning rather than political debates you can't fairly cover in 30 minutes.
- If someone asks something you don't know, say so and point them to a good source.
